Week 5 Discussion: The Cost of Industrialization

There are many hazards that differ across industries during industrialization. One being suffered by the Breaker Boys, in the coal mines, they constantly breathed in coal dust while sorting coal by hand. The dust was so thick that it often limited their vision. They did everything they could, like covering their faces with handkerchiefs (The Work of a Breaker Boy). In A Day in the Life of Young Angelo, he describes crouching on his hands and knees in front of coal, breathing coal dust through his handkerchief. Cotton girls faced a different kind of hazard. They worked long hours in hot, steamy mills where humidity had to be maintained to keep the cotton thread from breaking. Both environments, in their own right, present different environmental hazards and show how children’s health takes a back seat to production needs.

These conditions also connect to our 21st-century reality. In The Daily episode “A New Child Labor Crisis in America,” Hannah Dreier discusses migrant children working in factories, slaughterhouses, and construction tied to major American companies. This is connected to prior industrial labor because economic pressure can make families dependent on children's income. The ILO reports that child labor is more common in poorer households, although poverty is not the only cause. Modern supply chains are much more complex, making it easier to hide child labor deep within them. But basic problems remain; companies benefit from inexpensive labor at the expense of vulnerable families.

Early compulsory schooling and child labor laws failed to immediately eliminate child labor because enforcement was weak. For example, Pennsylvania required coal workers to be at least twelve years old. However, employers and sometimes parents would use fake documents to allow younger children to work (The Work of a Breaker Boy). Angelo furthers this argument. His father had a fake birth certificate notarized because the family relied on his wages. Moehling also found that minimum-age laws had little effect on children’s occupational choices at the start of the 20th century, suggesting that laws were weak without enforcement.
